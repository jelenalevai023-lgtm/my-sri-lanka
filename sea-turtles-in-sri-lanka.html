Sea turtles in Sri Lanka

From nesting beaches to hatcheries and conservation projects, sea turtles are one of the most memorable parts
of Sri Lanka’s coastline.

But seeing them is not quite the same as finding another attraction on a map.

Sea turtles have been coming to these shores for generations, returning from the ocean to lay their eggs on the same stretches of sand. Some appear in the water near the coast. Others come ashore after dark, disappearing again before sunrise.

And for a visitor, there is something almost unreal about that.

You can spend the whole day looking at the ocean without knowing that, somewhere beyond the waves, a turtle may be moving through a world
you cannot see.

Sri Lanka is home to five species of marine turtle: the green turtle, olive ridley, hawksbill, loggerhead and leatherback. All five are protected, and nesting beaches along parts of the southern and southeastern coast are especially
important for their survival.

Five visitors from the ocean

The green turtle is one of the species most closely associated with Sri Lanka’s coastline and is an important part of several conservation projects around the island.

Olive ridleys are the smallest of the five species found here, while hawksbills are known for their beautifully patterned shells.

Loggerheads are less commonly encountered by visitors, while the leatherback is impossible to mistake for any other sea turtle. It is the largest species of sea turtle in the world and looks very different from the others, with a distinctive leathery shell rather than the hard, scuted shell most people imagine when they think of a turtle.

The remarkable thing is that all five species use Sri Lanka’s coastline.

But that does not mean you will see all five.

Or even one.

Like every wildlife encounter, seeing a turtle depends on where you go, when you go and a fair amount of luck.

That is especially true when you are hoping to see them behaving naturally.

Where the journey begins

For many people, the most magical moment is not seeing a turtle swimming.

It is seeing one come ashore.

A female turtle can emerge from the sea at night, slowly make her way across the sand, dig a nest and lay her eggs before covering them carefully and returning
to the ocean.

The beach may look exactly the same the following morning.

Unless you know what to look for, you might walk past the tracks without realising what happened there during the night.

That is one of the reasons nesting beaches matter so much.

They are not simply beautiful parts
of the coast.

They are part of a much larger journey.

Scientific research has identified several important nesting areas along Sri Lanka’s southeastern coastline, including Rekawa Sanctuary and stretches of coast around Tangalle and Yala. A recent study found that the region still contains important nesting hotspots for all five species that
visit Sri Lanka.

Rekawa and the chance to see turtles in the wild

If you want to see nesting turtles, Rekawa is one of the places most closely connected with sea turtle conservation in Sri Lanka.

The beach was declared a sanctuary in 2006, and conservation work in the area has included long term monitoring and community participation. All five species found in Sri Lanka have been recorded nesting at Rekawa.

The experience is very different from walking through a hatchery.

There are no tanks to look into.

No guarantee that a turtle will appear.

You wait.

And if one does come ashore, the experience should happen with as little
disturbance as possible.

That means following the instructions of the people monitoring the beach, keeping a respectful distance and understanding that the turtle’s nesting comes

before your photograph.

If nothing appears that night, that is simply part of wildlife watching.

You came to see something wild.

Wildlife does not arrive on schedule.

Hatcheries and conservation

Sri Lanka also has several turtle hatcheries, particularly along the southern
and southwestern coast.

Places such as Kosgoda have become well known for giving visitors the opportunity to learn about sea turtles and see hatchlings at close range. Sri Lanka Tourism identifies Kosgoda as an important turtle conservation attraction and notes that turtle eggs can be found there throughout the year, although nesting
activity is seasonal.

But this is where the story
becomes more complicated.

A hatchery can play a role in conservation.

It can protect eggs from immediate threats, support rescue and rehabilitation work, educate visitors and involve local communities in protecting wildlife.

But not every place that allows tourists to see turtles works in exactly the same way.

Research into hatcheries in Sri Lanka has found that their contribution to conservation depends heavily on how they are managed. Responsible wildlife tourism also means avoiding unnecessary handling and disturbance, especially when animals are kept in captivity.

So if you visit a hatchery, look beyond the word conservation.

Ask what the project actually does.

Does it explain where the animals came from and why they are there?

Does it focus on education and protection?

Are visitors encouraged to observe rather than handle the animals?

Is there a clear reason why a turtle is being kept in care?

A good encounter should leave you knowing more about the animal than

when you arrived.

It should not simply give you a photograph.

The temptation to get closer

Baby turtles are almost impossible
not to love.

They are tiny.

They look surprisingly determined.

And when one begins moving towards the sea, every person nearby suddenly
wants to watch.

That is understandable.

But turtles do not need us to turn their journey into a performance.

The most responsible encounters are usually the simplest ones.

Watch quietly.

Keep your distance.

Do not touch a turtle unless you are specifically instructed to do so by
authorised conservation staff.

Do not use flash photography around nesting turtles or hatchlings.

And do not shine bright lights
directly at them.

Artificial light and disturbance can interfere with nesting behaviour and can also disorient hatchlings as they make their way towards the sea. Light pollution is recognised internationally as one of the significant threats to marine turtles
and nesting beaches.

Sometimes the best thing you can do is simply stand back.

Why responsible
encounters matter

Sea turtles have survived for millions of years.

And yet today, their lives are shaped by a long list of human pressures.

Fishing gear can accidentally catch and drown turtles.

Discarded nets and marine debris can entangle them.

Plastic can be mistaken for food.

Nesting beaches can be damaged by coastal development, artificial lighting and
poorly managed tourism.

Egg collection and illegal trade have also been serious threats.

These are not problems that belong to one beach or one country.

Marine turtles move across huge areas of ocean, which means protecting them often requires cooperation far beyond the place where they lay their eggs.

And that is why even a small wildlife encounter matters.

The way we choose to see an animal can either add to the pressure it already faces or help support the people and projects trying to protect it.

You might see one when you least expect it

Not every turtle encounter happens at a famous nesting beach.

Sometimes you may see one while swimming, snorkelling or diving.

Sometimes you may notice a dark shape moving slowly beneath the surface before
it disappears again.

And sometimes you may spend your entire holiday looking for one and never see anything at all.

That is wildlife.

There is no promise.

No perfect moment waiting for you at a certain time of day.

And perhaps that is what makes the rare encounters so memorable.

A turtle does not care that you travelled across the world to see it.

It is not there for you.

You simply happened to cross paths.

A coastline worth protecting

It is easy to think of the beach as
a place for us.

A place for swimming, walking, watching sunsets and doing absolutely nothing.

But after dark, the coastline becomes part of another world.

A turtle may emerge from the water.

Somewhere beneath the sand, eggs
may be developing.

Weeks later, tiny hatchlings may begin making their first journey towards the ocean.

Most people will never see any of it happen.

And maybe that is exactly why it feels so special when you do.

Sri Lanka’s sea turtles are not just another thing to add to an itinerary.

They are part of a much older story.

One that was happening long before the first hotel appeared beside the beach and one that will only continue if the places they return to are still there

when they need them.

If you are lucky
enough to see one

Watch quietly.

Give it space.

Put the flash away.

And remember that the best wildlife encounter is not always the closest one.

Sometimes it is the moment when you realise you are standing on the same beach, under the same night sky, while something ancient and completely indifferent to your presence continues its journey to the sea.

And for a few quiet minutes, you are simply lucky enough to witness it.
