Cook like a local

There is something different about eating Sri Lankan food once you have seen how
it is made.

Until then, a plate of rice and curry might look simple enough. Rice in the middle, a few colourful curries around it, maybe some sambol on the side. Delicious, of course, but you do not always realise how much is happening behind that plate.

A cooking class changes that.

You start noticing the spices, the coconut, the curry leaves and all the little steps that come together to create flavours you have been enjoying since the beginning of your trip. And suddenly, the food you have been eating everywhere makes a
little more sense.

It starts before you
enter the kitchen

One of the nicest things about taking a cooking class in Sri Lanka is that the experience can begin long before the first pan is placed on the stove.

Sometimes it starts with fresh ingredients laid out on a table. Sometimes with a walk through a local market. You look at vegetables you may have never seen before, smell unfamiliar spices and try to remember the names of everything you are about to cook with.

There is usually plenty of coconut too.

Fresh coconut is grated, squeezed and turned into coconut milk. Spices are prepared. Curry leaves appear. Chilli appears too, and probably more of it

than you expected.

By the time you finally step into the kitchen, you already understand that Sri Lankan food is not about throwing everything into one pot and hoping for the best.

There is a rhythm to it.

More than one curry

That is probably one of the first things you notice when you start cooking.

A typical Sri Lankan meal can include several different curries and side dishes, all prepared separately. There might be dhal bubbling away in one pan while vegetables are being cooked in another. Something is being fried, something is being tempered with spices and someone is preparing coconut sambol at
the same time.

Suddenly, that plate of rice and curry does not look quite so simple anymore.

Each dish has its own ingredients, its own spices and its own little process. Some flavours are rich and creamy from coconut milk. Others are fresh, sharp or spicy. And somehow, everything is meant
to work together.

It can feel like a little bit of chaos.

The good kind.

You are not just watching

The best cooking classes are not the kind where you sit in the corner and watch someone else do all the work.

You chop. You mix. You stir. You taste.

You might grate coconut for the first time and quickly discover that it takes more effort than it looks. You might add more chilli than you normally would at home. You might follow instructions perfectly and still end up asking if your curry is supposed to
look like that.

That is part of the fun.

A local kitchen is not a perfectly organised television set. There are bowls, pans, spices and ingredients everywhere. Things happen at the same time. Someone is always telling you
to stir something.

And somehow, by the end of it, there is food.

A lot of food.

The spices make sense once you see them

Sri Lankan cuisine is famous for its spices, but seeing them in the kitchen is very different from reading their names
on a menu.

You begin to understand how much flavour can come from a handful of simple ingredients. Mustard seeds, cumin, coriander, cinnamon, cardamom, cloves, turmeric, chilli and curry leaves can all play their part, often in ways that make one dish taste completely different from the next.

And then there is the smell.

That might be one of the things you remember most.

A kitchen filled with spices warming in a pan has a way of staying with you. It is the kind of smell that immediately makes you hungry, even if you have been eating little bits of everything throughout the entire class.

Coconut changes everything

If there is one ingredient you start to appreciate even more after cooking in Sri Lanka, it is coconut.

You see how many different ways it can be used. Freshly grated into sambol. Turned into coconut milk for curries. Added to sweets and other dishes. It appears again and again, each time bringing something slightly different to the table.

And that is another reason a cooking class is so much more interesting than simply finding a recipe online.

You can read about coconut milk. You can buy a can of it at home. But it is not quite the same as watching fresh coconut become part of almost everything
you are cooking.

Some things are easier to understand when you see them happen.

And then you sit down and eat

After all the chopping, stirring, tasting and wondering whether you added enough salt, the best part finally comes.

Everything goes onto the table.

There is rice, of course. Then all the curries and side dishes you have been making. The dhal. The vegetables. The sambol. Maybe something spicy enough to make you reach for your drink.

And you sit down and eat the food
you made together.

That is when it all comes together.

The ingredients you saw at the beginning of the day no longer feel unfamiliar. The spices have names now. You know what went into the curry. You remember which dish took the longest, which one surprised you and which one you will probably try to recreate when you get home.

Whether you actually succeed
is another question.

Take a little piece of
Sri Lanka home

You do not need to take a cooking class to eat well in Sri Lanka. Some of the best meals you will have might come from a tiny local restaurant, a beachside table or somewhere you stopped simply because
you were hungry.

But if you want to understand the food a little better, stepping into a local kitchen is one of the best ways to do it.

You do not have to become a chef.

You just have to be curious enough to ask what is in the pan, willing to get your hands a little messy and ready to eat far more than you planned.

And afterwards, when you sit down somewhere else in Sri Lanka and order rice and curry, you might look at that colourful plate a little differently.

Because now you know that there is a whole lot more behind it than

rice and curry.
